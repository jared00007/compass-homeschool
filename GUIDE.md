# Compass — The Parent's Guide

How the four agents decide what to teach, what only you can do, and what to watch
for during the test month before school starts.

*8th grade · age 13 · Washington state · ~10 minute read*

---

## The one idea to hold on to

Compass does not run your homeschool. It does two things: it decides **what comes
next** in the four core subjects, and it keeps the hour-by-hour record Washington
requires. Everything else — whether a lesson actually happened, whether he
understood it, what he wants to learn on his own — stays with you and him.

That split is the whole design. Get it wrong and you'll either fight the app or
trust it further than it deserves.

| | Who decides | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — core subjects** | The app | Math, Science, English, History. Each agent picks the next topic from his actual state, then writes the lesson. You approve, teach, and log it. |
| **Tier 2 — folded in** | The app | Reading, writing, spelling, art and music aren't separate lessons. They're earned inside Tier 1 work — a field journal entry is real writing. The agent claims those credits and must justify each one. |
| **Tier 3 — his choice** | **He does** | A list he curates, you approve. Coding, guitar, whatever he's into. No agent, no "optimal next step," no prerequisites. This is the counterweight to Tier 1's structure — protect it. |
| **Core life skills** | **You do** | Budgeting, cooking, changing a tire, writing a decent email. A checklist you maintain. Deliberately not agent-generated — and in practice where most Health and Occupational Education coverage comes from. |

## A normal day

Roughly fifteen minutes of your attention, most of it at the end rather than the start.

1. **Open Compass, read the home page.** *(30 seconds)* It shows what each of the four
   agents would teach next and why — computed instantly from his real state, no cost.
   Often that's all you need to plan the day.

2. **Generate the lessons you want.** *(the night before)* Pick a subject, set the
   length, add where you are if you're somewhere interesting. Generation takes 1–6
   minutes, so this is not something to do while he waits at the table.

3. **Read it before he does.** *(2 minutes)* Check the activities fit your day and the
   parent notes for anything you'd handle differently. You're the teacher; the lesson is
   a plan, not an instruction you're obliged to follow.

4. **He does the work.** *(your normal school time)*

5. **Log the hours — honestly.** *(1 minute, and the step that matters)* Edit the minutes
   to what it really took, adjust the subject credits if the agent over-claimed, save.
   This is your compliance record.

6. **Record math mastery when he's assessed.** *(assessment days only)* Nothing else
   unlocks the next math skill.

## How each agent decides

They're genuinely different, which is why there are four rather than one. Knowing each
one's logic tells you what it needs from you.

| Agent | How it picks | What it needs from you |
|---|---|---|
| **Math** | Walks a fixed 50-skill prerequisite map. A skill unlocks only when everything beneath it is mastered. | Recorded mastery. Without it, it starts him at integer operations. |
| **Science** | Pulls one thread and branches. Each lesson proposes 2–4 follow-ons that become the pool for next time. | Where you are. It searches the web for the real species and geology there. |
| **History** | Least-covered era on the timeline — unless where you are has a genuine connection, which wins. | Where you are, when it's somewhere with history worth standing in. |
| **English** | Anchored to the book he's actually reading, rotating through comprehension, vocabulary, writing, and craft. | The current book and roughly what page he's on. It refuses to run without one. |

Two of these need location; two don't. If you're parked somewhere ordinary, leave the
location blank — Science and History will teach a good at-home lesson rather than
forcing a weak local connection.

## The counting rule

This has to be right, and it's the thing most likely to confuse you on the compliance page.

> **Your total hours** come from how long each activity actually took. That's what counts
> toward the 1,000-hour requirement.
>
> **Per-subject hours** come from the credits on that activity — and they add up to *more*
> than the total. That's intentional.

One 75-minute nurse-log field study in the Hoh legitimately credits 75 minutes of science,
20 of math, 20 of writing, and 15 of art. That's 130 minutes of subject coverage from 75
minutes of his life. It's how you cover eleven subjects without running eleven subjects —
and it's exactly right, as long as each credit points at something he really did.

What you must not do is add the per-subject numbers together and call it your annual total.
That would inflate your year by more than half. The dashboard never does this, and neither
should you if a district asks.

## Math mastery is the gate

Everything the Math agent does depends on one thing: what you've marked mastered. Not
lessons generated, not hours logged — mastery.

Every math lesson ends with an assessment and a specific bar, like *"8 of 10 correct,
including at least 2 of the 3 applied problems."* Score it, then go to **Math → Record
mastery** and set the skill to mastered or leave it in progress. A skill left in progress
gets retaught next time rather than moving on.

> **Do this before school starts.** Sit down once and mark everything he already knows. It
> takes twenty minutes and costs nothing. Skip it and the agent will spend September
> teaching him to add negative numbers.

## The test month

Four weeks, and most of it isn't about the app working — it's about whether you trust what
it produces.

**Week 1 — Does it run, and is the output any good?**
Generate one lesson from each agent. Read them as a teacher, not a tester: would you
actually teach this? Is it pitched right for him? Roughly $1 total.

**Week 2 — Load his real state.**
Math mastery, the book he's reading, the life-skills checklist edited to what you care
about. Let him add three or four Tier 3 topics himself. Costs nothing.

**Week 3 — Run a full week as if it were school.**
Generate, teach, log, record mastery. This is where you find out whether the daily loop
fits your actual mornings, which is the real question.

**Week 4 — Check the record and adjust.**
Open the compliance page. Do the hours match reality? Are all eleven subjects getting
touched? Is any agent consistently over-claiming? Fix now, not in March.

## What to watch for

### Credit inflation

The single thing most worth your scepticism. When a science lesson claims 20 minutes of
writing, look at the activity it points to. A 250-word argument with cited evidence is
writing. A two-sentence caption is not.

This already happened once in testing: the Math agent billed five minutes of *language*
for "restating the definition in his own words" and ten minutes of *occupational education*
because a worked example used game-modding keybindings. Both were math instruction wearing
another subject's label. The rules were tightened and it stopped — but watch for the
pattern returning in a form nobody anticipated. Every credit is editable when you log the
activity.

### Location grounding that isn't real

Take Science or History somewhere you know well and check the specifics. Real place names,
real species, real dates — or plausible-sounding filler? It has been accurate in testing,
but you know your own ground better than any search result.

### Lessons that are impressive but not teachable

A six-activity lesson with a full answer key can still be wrong for a Tuesday with three
hours of driving in it. Cut it down. The lesson is a proposal.

## Housekeeping

### What it costs

| Agent | Per lesson | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Math | $0.17 | ~85 s |
| English | $0.20 | ~2 min |
| Science | $0.27 | ~3.5 min |
| History | $0.42 | ~6 min |

A school year runs roughly **$60–120** depending on how often you generate fresh rather
than reusing a lesson across sessions. The compliance page tracks your real spend, so you
never have to guess. Set a monthly cap in your Anthropic account regardless.

### Backups

Compass snapshots your record once a day automatically and keeps a month of them plus
monthly archives. But they sit on the same laptop as the original.

> **Do this once:** drag the `backups` folder into iCloud or Dropbox. A daily snapshot on
> the same disk does not survive losing the disk — and losing the disk means losing your
> year's documentation of instruction.

### Generation is slow, on purpose

History can take six minutes because it's genuinely researching. Plan tomorrow's lessons
tonight and it's a non-issue. Try to generate at the table and it's miserable.

## Mistakes that are easy to make

- **Logging the estimated time instead of the real time.** If a 60-minute lesson took 35,
  log 35. An inflated record is worse than a thin one.
- **Generating a fresh lesson every single session.** Most lessons carry across two or three
  sittings. This is the main thing separating a $60 year from a $120 one.
- **Skipping mastery recording.** The math sequence silently stalls. Nothing errors — it
  just keeps offering the same skill.
- **Letting Tier 3 become an assignment.** The moment you start picking his choice topics,
  it stops doing the job it exists for.
- **Treating the compliance dashboard as the truth rather than a mirror.** It reflects what
  you logged. Log carelessly and it will confidently show you a year that didn't happen.

## If something breaks

**An agent says it can't plan a lesson.** Deliberate, not a failure. English blocks when no
book is marked as currently reading. Math blocks if you force a skill whose prerequisites
aren't met — and shows the path to get there. The message tells you what to fix.

**A red banner about lesson generation.** The API key is missing or invalid. Everything else
keeps working — dashboard, log, mastery, choice topics, life skills. Fix the key when
convenient; you don't lose a day over it.

**You logged something wrong.** Delete it in the Activity Log and re-log it. If something
worse happens, restore yesterday's snapshot from **Compliance → Backups** — it saves your
current state first, so restoring is itself undoable.

---

Compass is a planning and record-keeping tool. It doesn't know whether a lesson landed,
whether he was engaged, or whether today was a day to close the books and go for a hike
instead. Those calls stay yours — the app just makes sure the year is documented when
someone asks.
